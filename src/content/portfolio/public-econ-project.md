*Group project (Group 5) with Anjali, Faheema and Shridhaya.*

## Tax morale and intergenerational preferences: how aging shapes support for public pension systems in India

We started with a simple question: why do some people feel responsible for paying taxes and supporting welfare, while others don't, and does that change as people get older?

Two ideas sit behind the title:

- **Tax morale** is how willing people are to pay taxes because they feel it's the right thing to do, not because they have to. It reflects trust, fairness and a sense of duty.
- **Intergenerational preferences** are about how one generation views supporting another: do younger people accept supporting the elderly, or do they see it as unfair?

## Why India

India's pension system has changed a lot. It began with a **Defined Benefit** system for government employees, with a guaranteed payout after retirement. In the early 2000s, the **National Pension System** moved new entrants to a **Defined Contribution** model, where what you get depends on what you contribute and how your investments perform. The aim was sustainability as the population ages. But much of the workforce, especially in the informal sector, still has no real pension coverage. We wanted to know how people actually feel about all this.

## What the literature says

We reviewed eight papers under four themes:

1. **Tax morale in India.** Korgaonkar (2022) finds willingness to pay depends on trust in institutions, satisfaction with public services and perceived fairness. Bhatia (2022) adds fairness, moral values and gender: people comply more when they believe the system is fair and others pay their share.
2. **Intergenerational preferences.** Tausch, Potters and Riedl (2013) review experiments showing people favour redistribution that guarantees a basic income without wrecking efficiency. Riekhoff (2020) finds that European pension reforms reduced support for pro-old policies but didn't produce a generational divide.
3. **Aging and pension attitudes.** Foster (2015) finds young UK adults are held back by low pension literacy, mistrust of providers and short-term money pressures. Bongaarts (2004) shows how aging strains pay-as-you-go systems in the G7.
4. **Governance and sustainability in India.** Yoganandham (2024) and Renjith and Ashkar trace the shift from defined-benefit to defined-contribution and argue sustainability depends on transparent, accountable governance.

## Our survey

We ran an anonymous online survey of voluntary participants, used only for academic purposes. It asked about three things:

- **Government spending priorities:** which areas matter most, where waste and corruption are worst, how far people trust the government to spend tax revenue wisely, and whether quality or coverage of public services matters more.
- **Pension attitudes:** Likert-scale statements on relying on private pensions, progressive pensions, raising the retirement age and adjusting pensions to life expectancy, plus universal versus targeted pensions.
- **Intergenerational preferences:** whether today's young taxpayers will get the same benefits as today's retirees, whether the system is financially sustainable, and whether it's fair for today's youth to fund current retirees while relying on future generations.

We analysed the answers with graphs, percentages and comparisons between age groups.

## What we found

**Government spending (all respondents, about 55):**
- Education (92.7%), healthcare (83.6%) and infrastructure (65.5%) were the top three priorities, out of three choices each.
- Infrastructure (83.6%) and social welfare (63.6%) were seen as suffering the most waste and corruption.
- Trust in government spending was moderate to slightly negative: the most common answers were 2 and 3 on a 5-point scale.
- 78.2% said improving the quality of public services matters more than expanding coverage.

**Pension attitudes.** Around half of each age group favoured a universal pension over a targeted one (50% of 18–44 and 47% of 45+), with 37% and 34% preferring targeting. Among the 45+ group, support for adjusting pension rules to life expectancy was broad. Views on raising the retirement age were mixed. Views on progressive pensions clustered around neutral.

**Intergenerational preferences.**
- Among 18–44s, 62% thought today's young taxpayers wouldn't get the same pension benefits as current retirees, 87% thought it unfair that today's youth fund current retirees while relying on future generations, and 75% thought the current system isn't financially sustainable.
- Among those 45 and over, 55% doubted that young taxpayers will benefit, and 79% thought the current system isn't sustainable in the long term.

In short, both age groups doubt the system's long-term sustainability and fairness across generations. Older respondents seemed more engaged with pension policy, while younger respondents' views were more divided.

## Limitations

The survey is small and not representative. Of about 55 respondents, roughly 71% were aged 45–59 and only about eight were aged 18–44, so the comparisons between younger and older groups rest on a very small younger sample. The sample also skews male and highly educated (around two thirds were postgraduates). Because the survey is cross-sectional, it can't separate the effect of age from differences between generations. The findings are suggestive rather than conclusive.

## Where it could go next

Informal-sector workers, who often have no pension coverage, are exactly the group this survey didn't reach. Understanding how they feel about taxes and pensions would add a lot of depth.
